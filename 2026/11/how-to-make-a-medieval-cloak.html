How to Make a Medieval Cloak for Renaissance Faires

By Just Halloween Crafts. Published November 19, 2026

To make a medieval cloak, choose a matte natural fabric such as wool or a wool blend, cut it as a half circle (or a simple rectangle for an earlier, plainer look), add a hood if you want one, hem the edges and fasten it at the front with a metal clasp, a brooch or two ties. A half-circle cloak in about 3 yards (2.7 m) of wide fabric is the most useful shape for a Renaissance faire because it drapes in soft folds, covers the arms and works over almost any costume.

The steps below cover choosing fabric, sizing and cutting both shapes, adding a hood, and the small changes that make a cloak practical for a long day outdoors, including a damp one. If you have never made a cloak before, our basic tutorial on how to sew a cloak without a pattern explains the same half-circle method with photos.

What You Need

  • Fabric: about 3 yards (2.7 m) of 58 to 60 inch (150 cm) wide wool, wool blend, heavy linen or cotton canvas for an adult ankle-length cloak; add about 1 yard (1 m) for a hood
  • Matching thread (polyester or cotton-wrapped polyester is strong enough for wool)
  • Fabric scissors, a tape measure, chalk or a fabric marker, and pins or sewing clips
  • A long piece of string and a pencil for drawing curves
  • A sewing machine or a needle for hand sewing
  • A closure: a metal cloak clasp, a large brooch or cloak pin, or cord for ties
  • Optional: lining fabric, and bias binding for the neckline

Which Fabric Is Best for a Medieval Cloak?

Wool is the best fabric for a medieval cloak because it looks right, drapes heavily and stays fairly warm even when it gets damp. Choose a matte finish: shiny satin and stretchy fabrics read as costume rather than period clothing.

Earthy colors such as forest green, brown, rust, grey, dark red and deep blue all suit a medieval look. Our full comparison of the best fabric for a cloak or cape covers velvet, cotton and more if you are still deciding.

Step 1: Choose a Cloak Shape and Measure

Pick a half-circle cloak for most faire costumes, or a rectangle cloak if you want the plainest, earliest style that is pinned at one shoulder.

  1. Length: measure from the bump at the back of the neck to where the cloak should end. For a faire, stop about 2 inches (5 cm) above the ankle bone so the hem stays out of mud and is not stepped on.
  2. Neck: measure around the base of the neck and add 2 inches (5 cm) so the cloak sits loosely.
  3. For a rectangle cloak: the width should reach from wrist to wrist with the arms held out, and the length from the shoulder to the knee or calf.

Not sure how full you want it? Our guide to quarter, half and full circle cloaks shows how much fabric each shape needs.

Step 2: Cut the Cloak

A half-circle cloak is cut as one large curved piece; a rectangle cloak needs no curves at all.

  1. Half circle: fold the fabric in half so the cut edges meet. Divide the neck measurement by 3.14 to get the neck radius. Tie the string to the chalk, hold the other end at the folded corner and draw a small quarter curve at the neck radius, then a large curve at the neck radius plus the cloak length. Cut along both curves through both layers.
  2. If your fabric is not wide enough for the full length, cut two quarter circles and sew them together along the center back.
  3. Rectangle: simply cut a rectangle to your measurements and round the bottom corners slightly so they hang neatly.
Tip: Wool shrinks when washed in warm water. If you plan to wash the finished cloak by hand, soak and dry the fabric the same way before you cut it.

Step 3: Add a Hood (Optional)

A hood is the most useful addition for an outdoor faire, and it is sewn separately and then attached to the neckline.

Follow our guide on how to make a hood for a cloak or cape for a rounded hood, or use the shape in how to make a pointed hood for the long-tailed look of a medieval liripipe. Make the hood bottom edge match the cloak's neckline, then sew the two together with the right sides facing. Our hooded cloak tutorial shows that join step by step.

Step 4: Finish the Edges

Hem every edge so the cloak hangs heavily and does not fray during a long day.

  1. On the curved bottom edge, fold up 1/2 inch (12 mm) and press, then fold another 1/2 inch and stitch. A narrow hem lies flat on a curve; a deep one puckers.
  2. Fold and stitch the two straight front edges with a 1 inch (2.5 cm) hem.
  3. Finish the neckline with bias binding, or sew on the hood if you made one.
  4. For a warmer or more finished cloak, add a lining before hemming. Our guide on how to line a cloak or cape explains the method.

Step 5: Add a Clasp, Brooch or Ties

Medieval-style cloaks are usually closed with a metal clasp at the throat or a brooch, and a rectangle cloak is often pinned at the right shoulder so the right arm stays free.

  1. Cut two small squares of interfacing or spare fabric and sew them inside the front corners to reinforce them.
  2. Sew one half of the clasp to each front corner, going through every hole several times.
  3. For a brooch or cloak pin, pin through both layers of the reinforced corners instead, so the pin does not tear the fabric.

Our guide on how to tie or fasten a cloak or cape compares clasps, ties and frog closures and shows how to sew each one.

Making the Cloak Practical for a Rainy Faire

For a wet day, wool or a thick wool blend is the most comfortable choice, because a fully waterproof cloak traps heat and gets clammy on a long walk.

  • Keep the hem a little shorter than usual so it does not soak up water from wet grass.
  • Add a hood that is deep enough to come forward over the forehead.
  • Choose a darker color, which hides mud splashes.
  • Let a damp wool cloak dry flat or on a wide hanger, away from radiators and direct heat.

Safety Notes

Keep any cloak away from candles, torches and fire pits, which are common at faires, and take extra care with polyester fabrics, which melt. Fasten children's cloaks with hook-and-loop tabs rather than ties around the neck, and keep their hems well above the ankle so they cannot trip.

What is a medieval cloak made of?

Wool is the traditional choice for a medieval cloak because it is warm, drapes well and copes with damp weather. For a costume, wool or a wool blend looks the most authentic, heavy linen suits hot summer faires, and cotton canvas is a sturdy budget choice. Avoid shiny satin if you want a period look.

How much fabric do I need for a medieval cloak?

An adult ankle-length half-circle cloak needs about 3 yards (2.7 m) of 58 to 60 inch wide fabric, plus about 1 yard (1 m) for a hood. A plain rectangle cloak to the knee needs roughly 2 yards (1.8 m). Measure the wearer first, because height changes the amount a lot.

How do you wear a medieval cloak?

Settle the cloak on both shoulders and close it at the front with a clasp or ties just below the throat. A rectangle cloak is usually wrapped around the body and pinned at the right shoulder with a brooch, leaving the right arm free. Pull the hood up for rain or cold.

Can I make a medieval cloak without sewing?

Yes, for a simple version. Cut a rectangle of wool or fleece, which do not fray much, and pin it at the shoulder with a large brooch. For a half-circle cloak, use iron-on hemming tape or fabric glue for the edges. Our no-sew cloak guide shows the method.

Once the cloak is done, finish the outfit with a DIY robe underneath or a rustic broom prop, and see our hooded cloak guide for a fuller fantasy version. You'll find more cloak, cape and hood tutorials in our costume accessories section.
